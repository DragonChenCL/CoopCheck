Chicken nesting box calculator: how many per hen?

Calculate how many nesting boxes you need for 2–100 laying hens. One 12-inch nest for about four hens, plus layout and placement advice from Extension.

How many nesting boxes for your hens?

Oregon State University Extension suggests one nest box for about every four laying hens. Boxes can be shared, so a flock of six hens does not need six private boxes. Enter your adult hen count below and round up to the next whole box. A typical starting interior size per standard-hen nest is 12 × 12 × 12 inches.

Box placement matters more than extra count

Give birds a quieter laying area that is accessible for egg collection and cleaning. OSU Extension advises against placing boxes directly on the ground and says nests should be lower than the roosting poles, so birds are less likely to sleep in them. A sloped top discourages sleeping above nest boxes.

If you keep heavy breeds or special breeds, adjust physical box size and access instead of treating 12 inches as an exact build specification.

The box count is easy; placement and daily access are the real decisions

The one-box-per-four-laying-hens starting ratio is a planning convention, not a guarantee of quiet nesting or an ordinance. Count the adult hens expected to lay, not every animal in a mixed-age flock. For an unknown future laying flock, write down the assumed final number and revisit the count as birds mature.

Moving from 12 laying hens to 13 raises the worksheet from three to four boxes. That one-bird threshold affects your layout more than merely changing a number on a calculator.

Draw the nest front, not only the box footprint

Utah State University Extension describes nest width and height around 12–15 inches, at least 12 inches of depth, a 4–6-inch front lip and dry bedding; it also describes roughly one box for four to five hens. These are extension suggestions, not exact dimensions for every breed or an engineered design. Three boxes each with 12-inch-wide openings need at least 36 inches of nominal opening width before partitions, fasteners or keeper access are allowed for. A fourth adds another 12-inch opening before those allowances.

Prevent a mathematically correct but impractical nest wall

  1. Show how eggs can be reached without crawling behind a perch or through droppings.
  2. Keep roosts positioned so litter and manure do not fall into nests; the highest cozy box may become a sleeping spot.
  3. Plan dry, replaceable bedding and an entry that works for the bird size in your flock.
  4. Ask whether an external nest projection changes the building or setback dimensions; it does not automatically increase bird floor.

Cross-check with the independent perch-length calculator and the USU nest construction and placement notes.

Use the result to reserve accessible nest positions. It does not predict even use, settle a breed-specific size or guarantee clean eggs. Check collection access, entrance clearance and whether the nest structure permanently blocks bird floor.

Before adding boxes to solve a problem, record what happens: which boxes are occupied, where eggs are laid and whether entrances are obstructed. Take the observation record to a poultry adviser; this count tool does not diagnose flock behaviour. The sources concern laying females, not a universal fixture requirement for meat birds.
