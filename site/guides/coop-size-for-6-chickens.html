Going from four hens to six: what actually needs to change?

Audit the extra indoor area, run, nest and perch needed when growing a four-hen flock to six. Worked upgrade arithmetic and a no-purchase-yet checklist.

Adding two hens does not mean adding just one small box. Check the existing house, outdoor enclosure and access route separately before expanding the flock.

An upgrade ledger, not a shopping list

Using the existing daily-run-access planning references, six hens need an indoor comparison of 24 sq ft and a separate outdoor comparison of 60 sq ft. The table isolates what changes from a four-hen setup; it does not assume the old building was adequate or that the city permits six birds.

Check the weakest component first

Suppose the existing interior is 4 x 6 ft, but a fixed feed cupboard makes two square feet inaccessible. There are 22 usable square feet, so the indoor comparison is still two square feet below 24. Buying more fencing does not correct that indoor shortfall.

Conversely, a clear 24-square-foot house beside a 5 x 8 ft run meets the selected indoor target but has only 40 of the 60 outdoor square feet. Extending the run to 5 x 12 ft adds the missing 20. These are hypothetical measurements, not a judgement about a retail model.

An extension must connect to usable space

Draw the doorway joining the old and new areas. An added rectangle behind an inaccessible partition is not usable bird floor. Record opening width, obstructions, weather protection and how you would reach the extension for cleaning. Do not add the wire run to the sleeping-house total just because the units share a door.

Use the two-nest figure to place entrances, not to promise that hens will share them evenly. For perches, total the unobstructed usable lengths; a five-foot bar is not five feet of bird space if its ends are blocked by a wall fixture.

Do not use construction arithmetic as an introduction plan

New birds also raise flock-management and health questions that these tools do not assess. Confirm an introduction and separation plan with a suitable local poultry adviser before acquiring birds. A pass on square footage is not evidence that an established flock will accept newcomers.

Keep a short decision record: current clear floor; current run; local flock limit; intended alterations; source or city reply. Stop the purchase if the local six-bird allowance or the available space remains unknown.

Six hens: distinguish overnight sleeping accommodation from the all-day housing envelope

Six standard laying hens multiplied by the planner's 4 sq ft target produces a 24 sq ft indoor design reference, plus a separate 60 sq ft run. OSU Extension instead describes a 3 sq ft per bird minimum with outside access (18 sq ft for six) and 8-10 sq ft per bird without outside access (48-60 sq ft). These figures are not interchangeable: change the birds' access and the comparison changes. Source: Backyard chicken coop design.

A manufacturer may quote the count of birds that can roost overnight, even if a standalone six-bird enclosure cannot support all-day confinement. For example, Omlet's published comparison describes the Eglu Cube as housing six large hens with an internal width/depth rectangle of 34 x 33 inches. That is about 7.79 sq ft by rectangle arithmetic, but neither the published count nor the rectangle alone establishes the full daytime-use arrangement. Manufacturer statement.

Compare proposals, not only numbers: write down how many hours the birds can access the run, what happens during sustained rain or snow, how two nests and 4.5 ft of combined roost would be positioned, and which floor patches are inaccessible after equipment is installed. Ask for the effective inner floor; use the prefab buyer checker only after recording the correct scenario.
