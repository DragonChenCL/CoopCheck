How far should a chicken coop be from your property line?

There is no single nationwide distance. Some cities measure from the coop to the lot boundary; others measure to a neighboring residential building or apply both rules. Before buying a coop, find out which one your city actually uses.

Quick answer: check the measurement target first

A setback is the minimum separation required by an applicable local rule. The rule might name a side property line, front lot line, rear lot line, neighboring residence, or the area of the entire livestock enclosure. Those are not interchangeable.

For example, Portland's current City Code §13.40.040(D) requires structures in a livestock facility to sit at least 3 feet from side and rear property lines and 10 feet from the front property line. Extra separation applies on lots with more than one residential unit. By contrast, Seattle's official Urban Agriculture guidance calls for structures housing domestic fowl to be at least 10 feet from a residential structure on an adjacent lot. Seattle may also have other yard and accessory-structure requirements. Neither example is a rule for other cities.

Important: A backyard fence is not proof of the legal property line. The city code may also regulate the outdoor run, roof overhangs, other structures, zoning, permits and private HOA restrictions. Get actual boundaries from reliable property or survey records before building.

How to check a real coop position before buying

  1. Confirm jurisdiction: make sure you are inside the named city, not an unincorporated county area. Get your lot zoning and residential unit count.
  2. Find the right legal sections: read animal-keeping limits and accessory-structure/zoning chapters. Ask about coop and run, permit, roosters, and any city-specific exemptions.
  3. Identify the measurement origin: boundary, building, neighboring dwelling, yard or entire enclosure. A backyard fence is not necessarily the surveyed boundary.
  4. Draw your lot: note the house, shed, coop, run, gates and adjacent residential buildings. Mark each rule's measurement target separately.
  5. Confirm with city staff: if the current code or your lot layout is ambiguous, ask the planning/building or animal-control department before construction.
Do not use a “fit” result as a permit decision. Even if a rectangle has room for the coop, the location can be restricted by zoning, use, enclosure size, sanitation, adjoining buildings, easements or HOA covenants.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put a chicken coop next to the fence?

Not safely on the basis of a fence alone. A fence might not follow the legal property line, and your city may regulate distance from structures or neighbors. Use the current local code and verified boundary information.

Is 10 feet the standard chicken coop setback?

No. Seattle's domestic-fowl rule refers to distance from neighboring residential structures; Portland specifies different property-line distances. Other cities and lot types vary. There is no generic 10-foot nationwide rule.

Are chicken runs measured separately?

It depends on the ordinance's definitions of facility, structure, enclosure and run. Ask whether it applies to the coop roof, enclosed run or whole livestock facility before measuring.

Does a free spacing result mean my coop is legal?

No. This worksheet tests only a rectangular footprint against example buffers that you enter. CoopCheck's free map tool is also a planning sketch, not an official survey or municipal approval.
