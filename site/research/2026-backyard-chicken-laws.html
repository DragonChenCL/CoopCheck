The finding: an answer needs more than a chicken count.

Local rules often distinguish a permitted flock from the legality of a particular coop location. A hen limit can change with lot size, the structure's distance to a property line, animal type, or zoning. This is an evidence inventory of selected cities—not a random national survey and not a list of universally approved coop plans.

How to read the numbers. “Source-reviewed” means the profile passed CoopCheck's existing editorial-source threshold; it does not mean all four fields were resolved. “Field checked” means a specific statement has a recorded primary clause or city guidance reference. It does not mean the whole property is legally compliant. Do not report these dataset shares as percentages of all U.S. cities.

Four questions, not one

  1. How many hens or mixed domestic fowl can a household keep?
  2. Are roosters or drakes treated differently?
  3. Which animal, zoning or accessory-building permits may apply?
  4. Where may the coop and run go relative to lot lines or neighboring homes?

Planning a coop from a generic “X feet from the fence” figure can be misleading when the rule refers instead to a neighboring residence or depends on lot size.

Five local examples that show why details matter

The examples below were independently cross-checked against official government information on October 22, 2026. They illustrate differences, not a representative distribution.

Portland, OR — lot-size thresholds

Generally four small domestic fowl on any lot; six on lots of at least 10,000 sq ft. Additional allowances and land-use exceptions apply; its city guidance says an animal-keeping permit is not required.

Portland Code §13.40.020 ↗ · Local profile
San Diego, CA — distance changes the limit

Five, fifteen, or twenty-five chickens can apply under different coop distance conditions. The city specifically prohibits roosters; applicants must check the parcel's actual zoning setbacks.

San Diego official guidance ↗ · Local profile
Denver, CO — a distinct permit system

Denver's Food-Producing Animals permit covers up to eight hens and ducks combined with no roosters or drakes. The city states a one-time $25 per-property permit fee.

Denver official program ↗ · Local profile
Chandler, AZ — old guidance can conflict

Chandler's December 2024 Ordinance 5113 updated the hen number from five to six for qualifying residential properties, while retaining no-rooster and other coop conditions. Older handouts may still mention five.

Adopted ordinance memo ↗ · Local profile
Seattle, WA — lot use can matter

Seattle generally allows eight domestic fowl, prohibits roosters, and describes additional allowances for qualifying larger lots. Checking the land-use category remains essential.

Seattle animal code guidance ↗ · Local profile
What is a useful citation?

A cited ordinance section, the actual city, the conditions it applies to, and the evidence review date. A generic chicken-count chart without exceptions is not enough to approve a coop plan.

How many chickens can you have inside city limits?

There is no single U.S. city-limit chicken allowance. Some cities use a fixed household limit, while others vary the number by lot size, zoning district, coop placement, or permits. Here is a practical way to check your own property before buying birds or building a coop.

1. Identify your exact city jurisdiction

Your mailing address and your local government are not always the same. Check whether the property sits inside city limits or in an unincorporated county area; their chicken ordinances can differ.

2. Find the permitted number—and the conditions

For example, Portland, Oregon generally allows four small domestic fowl on any lot and six on qualifying lots of at least 10,000 sq ft, subject to further exceptions. San Diego, California describes different chicken counts depending on enclosure placement. Neither rule applies nationwide.

3. Check the coop, not only the hens

Look separately for rooster restrictions, animal permits, accessory-structure permits, property-line setbacks, and distances to neighboring residences. HOA covenants or rental agreements may add private restrictions.

Methodology, limitations and reuse

How the evidence inventory was assembled

The source is CoopCheck's versioned municipal city registry and field-evidence register. The recorded review was last updated October 13, 2026. Each of the four fields is exported only when the city was eligible for publication and its evidence has primary_clause_checked status without a rejected claim. Pending or incomplete fields have empty summaries in the CSV, not guessed values.

Five illustrative jurisdictions above received a fresh October 22 cross-check against government guidance. The remainder retain the repository's earlier recorded evidence level; this release is not a full October 22 re-audit of every ordinance.

What the data cannot tell you

This is a nonrandom, project-selected list of 100 cities. It cannot support claims such as “X% of U.S. cities ban roosters.” Source-checked fields can be conditional or limited in scope; zoning district, lot size, HOA covenants, code updates, nuisance rules and building approvals may change a parcel-level answer. Check official local authorities before construction or livestock purchase.

October 2026 local-policy watch: Winter Springs, Florida

Winter Springs illustrates a key distinction for coop buyers: a city can approve backyard hens while still making eligibility depend on the lot and the structure.

On October 27, Oviedo Community News reported that Winter Springs approved a backyard chicken program after the October 26 City Commission second-reading hearing for Ordinance 2026-08. The report describes hen-only keeping, lot-dependent flock limits, and requirements for coop placement and screening. A coop larger than 120 square feet may trigger a building permit, according to the same report.

Verification boundary: this is a news-based policy watch, not a newly audited city profile or a legal determination. The final adopted ordinance text, parcel zoning, HOA restrictions and current city permitting guidance should be checked with Winter Springs before buying birds or beginning construction. Winter Springs is not an added member of the 100-city sample or the downloadable evidence matrix. This section does not change the study's coverage totals.
