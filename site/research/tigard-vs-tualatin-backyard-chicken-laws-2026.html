Tigard vs. Tualatin: two different approaches to backyard fowl

Neighbors on the map, not twins in the law. One city's current ordinance targets nuisances and the conditions of keeping livestock; the other's 2026 framework requires a license and limits qualifying household flocks to four domestic fowl.

Published October 23, 2026 · CoopCheck Research · Primary municipal sources and independently labeled reporting

The verified distinction: Tigard's 2022 nuisance-code approach does not set a general numeric chicken cap in the reviewed city livestock chapter, but it does prohibit older roosters and regulate animal facilities. In Tualatin, a 2026 city amendment allows a combined total of four domestic fowl in eligible Low Density Residential areas with a renewable five-year license. Neither conclusion is a permit or legal clearance for a specific parcel. Check the original text.

How Tigard got here: a sourced timeline

2018
City Ordinance 18-04 repealed Tigard Municipal Code §6.02.070 (livestock/poultry). Existing general nuisance rules still applied. Official ordinance disposition table.
Feb 2022
The council's official recap acknowledged neighborhood complaints about chickens and rats and said staff were reviewing urban livestock code. Official February 22 council recap.
Mar 2022
Tigard Life documented a specific neighborhood dispute that helped spur public debate. Its reporting provides historical context; private allegations are not judicial findings. Local reporting.
Nov 1, 2022
City Council adopted Ordinance 22-09, a comparatively moderate approach centered on nuisance prevention, rooster restrictions, shelter and feeding standards, rather than the stricter proposed numerical-cap model. Official council recap.
Apr 22, 2026
Nearby Tualatin's more prescriptive domestic-fowl rules took effect: four permitted birds, a renewable license, and explicit coop setback and facility-size standards. Official city guidance.

Why the comparison matters

The same backyard plan can face different legal questions

A four-hen coop in Tigard raises questions about nuisance prevention, shelter conditions, sanitation, zoning and construction rules. In Tualatin the same idea additionally encounters a defined four-bird combined cap, geographic zoning eligibility, a five-year domestic-fowl license and different coop distances. Neither city should be described as wholly “unregulated,” and Tualatin's permit rules cannot be exported to Tigard.

A second, frequently missed distinction: keeping livestock and constructing a building are not necessarily the same permission. Homeowners should seek current property-specific guidance from the appropriate city before construction or stocking a flock.
